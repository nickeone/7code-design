Life is Hard

Two roles open for five months, and the roadmap keeps moving right

You signed off two senior hires in the spring. One shortlist evaporated when the best candidate took a counter-offer. The second went quiet after the technical stage. Meanwhile the roadmap has slipped a quarter, then another quarter, and your board deck now has a slide explaining why.

Or you already tried an agency. Three CVs arrived, two of those people never appeared on a call, and the one who did needed six weeks of hand-holding before writing anything you kept. You paid for all six weeks. Nobody wants to repeat that conversation internally.

The pattern underneath both is the same. You do not have a talent problem. You have a throughput problem, and every route to fixing it either takes five months or hands you someone you never met.

We solve the second one first. You interview the engineer. The engineer you interview is the engineer who starts. If that is not true, nothing else on this page matters.

The roles we staff

  • Senior backend engineers — Node, Python, Go, .NET, Java. People who have owned a service in production, not people who have contributed to one.
  • Senior frontend and full-stack engineers — React, Next.js, TypeScript. Comfortable with design systems, accessibility and the parts of the job that are not the happy path.
  • Software architects — for teams whose codebase has outgrown its original decisions. Someone to own the shape of the system while your leads keep shipping features.
  • AI and ML engineers — LLM and agent development, retrieval pipelines, evals, guardrails and observability. Engineers who have put this in production, not in a notebook.
  • DevOps and platform engineers — CI/CD, infrastructure as code, cloud cost, release engineering. The work that quietly decides how fast everyone else can move.
  • Data engineers — pipelines, warehousing, system integrations between tools that were never designed to talk to each other.
  • QA automation engineers — test strategy and suites your team will still be running in a year.
  • Full pods — an architect plus two or three senior engineers, delivering a whole area of your product. See AI product engineering for the work we own end to end.

The six commitments, and what each one protects you from

  • You interview them, and who you interview is who you get. Every candidate does a technical interview with your team. No profile swaps after signature, no "that engineer became unavailable" the week before start. This kills the single biggest failure mode in this market.
  • They join your team, not a parallel one. Your repo, your board, your standups, your code review process, your definition of done. We do not run a separate project plan and report against it. You manage the work exactly as you manage your own engineers.
  • First pull request inside two weeks of start. Usually sooner. We plan the first fortnight with your lead before day one, so the engineer arrives with a ticket and an environment rather than a reading list. Ramp-up is our problem, not something you pay for.
  • Two to four weeks from contract to a working team. Not a pipeline to open, a bench to allocate from. If we cannot staff your stack in that window, we tell you on the first call instead of stringing you along while we recruit against your requirement.
  • Monthly rolling, not a twelve-month lock-in. One month's notice, either side, from either of us. We hold engagements by being worth keeping. If the commercial model is the only thing holding a supplier in place, the delivery was never the point.
  • You can end it, and you keep everything. Code, documentation, architecture decisions and context stay with you. We run a written handover as standard, not as a paid extra at the unhappy end of a relationship.

Our point of view

Staff augmentation gets a bad name because most of the market sells access to people rather than accountability for outcomes. Three CVs, a rate card, an invoice. Whether the work landed is treated as your management problem.

We think the opposite. An engineer we place is an engineer whose output we are answerable for. That is why we insist you interview them, why we plan the first two weeks before day one, and why the contract is monthly. Each of those makes it harder for us to hide behind a signed term. That is the point.

It also means we say no more often than is commercially convenient. If your stack is one we cannot staff at a genuinely senior level, we will tell you on the first call rather than sending a near-enough profile and hoping.

Who we are not for: if you want the cheapest hourly rate on the market, we are the wrong partner. Our engineers cost less than a UK contractor and considerably more than the bottom of the global market. Teams who buy on rate alone should buy elsewhere, and usually do it twice.

Questions we get asked before we're hired

What does a dedicated engineer actually cost per month?

A dedicated senior engineer is typically €6,400–€8,000 per month, full time and on your team only. A dedicated engineer at architect level is usually €9,600–€12,800 per month. A dedicated pod of an architect plus two senior engineers typically sits between €22,000 and €29,000 per month. What moves the number inside those bands is stack scarcity, the seniority you need and whether the role requires specific domain experience. There is no setup fee and no recruitment fee on top. More detail in what staff augmentation costs.

Can we interview the engineers before they join?

Yes, and we would rather you did. You get CVs, then a technical interview with your own team, run your way. You can set a take-home, pair on a real ticket, or grill someone on their last production incident. You decide. The commitment behind it matters more than the interview itself: the engineer you interview is the engineer who starts. We do not substitute profiles after signature.

What happens if it is not working out?

Tell us early and we replace the engineer at our cost, or you end the engagement. Contracts are monthly rolling with one month's notice on either side. There is no twelve-month minimum, no exit fee and no clause that makes leaving expensive. In practice the problem is usually visible inside three weeks, which is exactly why we do not ask you to commit for a year.

What timezone do your engineers work in, and how much overlap do we get?

We deliver from Cluj-Napoca, Romania, which is UTC+2 in winter and UTC+3 in summer. That is a two-hour offset from the UK. Your 9am standup is their 11am, and their day runs past yours. In practice it is a full working-day overlap, so there is no follow-the-sun handover and no waiting overnight for an answer. Read more on nearshore software development.

Do they work in our repo and follow our process?

Yes. Your repo, your CI, your ticketing, your code review rules, your definition of done. Engineers join your standups and your retros as team members. We do not run a shadow project plan or send you a weekly status report about work you can already see in your own board. If your process is the thing that needs fixing, we will say so, but we will still follow it first.

Who owns the IP, and will you sign our NDA?

You own everything the engineer produces, assigned to you under the contract and transferring on full payment. That covers code, documentation, designs and models. We sign your NDA and your MSA if you have them, and we have our own if you do not. Every engineer is under a personal confidentiality and IP assignment agreement with us, so the chain of title holds end to end. As an EU company we are GDPR-bound and we will sign a DPA before anyone gets access.

How is this different from using a recruiter?

A recruiter finds you a person, takes a percentage of first-year salary and leaves. From that moment the employment risk, the notice period, the sick cover and the replacement cost are yours. We employ the engineer. If they leave us, replacing them is our problem and our cost, not a second fee to you. You also get to stop on a month's notice, which is not something a permanent hire gives you. If a permanent hire is genuinely the right answer for the role, we will say that on the first call.

What happens to the knowledge when an engineer rolls off?

This is the right question to ask, and it is why we push pods rather than lone engineers on anything long-running. Practically: architecture decisions are written down as decision records in your repo, not held in someone's head. Pull requests are reviewed by at least one other person who understands the area. Where a single engineer holds a critical area, we overlap them with a second person before any planned exit. And on roll-off we run a written handover to whoever you nominate. None of that is an extra line on the invoice.

How quickly can someone actually start?

Two to four weeks from signed contract to an engineer in your standup is the normal range, and it covers interviews, notice and account provisioning. Common stacks at the shorter end, scarce specialisms at the longer. We will give you a date on the first call and tell you if the honest answer is longer. You can see how we work for the full sequence.

We used an outsourcing supplier before and it went badly. Why is this different?

Most of the stories we hear share three causes: you never met the person, the contract was long enough that the supplier stopped trying, and the engineer was managed by the supplier rather than by you. We have removed all three deliberately. You interview. The term is monthly. The engineer reports into your team lead. That does not guarantee a good outcome, but it means a bad one surfaces in week three rather than month nine, and you can act on it.

Where does our code and data actually sit?

Inside your own environment. Engineers work in your cloud accounts and your repos, under your access controls, and we ask for the least access that lets them do the job. We are an EU company delivering from Romania, so any data we do handle stays inside the EEA under GDPR, covered by a DPA. If you need device policies, VPN-only access or a specific security posture, that gets agreed before start rather than discovered afterwards.

What if what we actually need is a whole product, not extra hands?

Then we will tell you that. Sometimes the honest read is that the team is not short of capacity, it is short of a defined scope. In that case a fixed-scope build such as a Production MVP is the better shape, and we run those too. We also build AI automation where the real bottleneck turns out to be an operational process rather than the product backlog. ---
