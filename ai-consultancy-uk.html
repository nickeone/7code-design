Life is Hard

You are not short of AI ambition. You are short of a costed answer

Three departments are each running their own pilot. Operations has a tool on a corporate card, finance is trialling something a vendor demoed well, and someone in marketing has been quietly productive with a chatbot for months. Nobody has costed any of it, nobody owns it, and none of it is on a risk register.

Meanwhile the board has asked for an AI strategy by the next meeting. Or worse, money has already gone out — a pilot, a platform licence, a consultant's deck — and there is nothing to put in front of them that shows a return.

The problem is not that your team lacks ideas. It is that no one has priced them. AI opportunities all look equally attractive until someone works out what each costs to build, what it costs to run per month, what it saves, and what happens when it gets an answer wrong in front of a customer.

That is the work. It takes weeks, not quarters, and it ends in a ranked list with numbers next to it — including the items we recommend you do not fund.

What you get, and what we build after it

  • A costed opportunity map — every AI and automation candidate in your business, with a build cost, a monthly running cost, an estimated return and a recommendation. Including the ones to stop.
  • A pilot audit — what the three departments are already running, what it costs, what data it touches, and which of it belongs on a risk register today rather than after an incident.

  • [LLM and agent development](/service/llm-agent-development) — assistants and agents that read, decide and act inside your systems, with evals, guardrails, traces and cost controls from day one.
  • [System integrations](/service/system-integrations) — the connective work that makes any of it possible, and the part most strategy decks skip because it is not interesting to write about.
  • [Cloud infrastructure](/service/cloud-agentic-infra) — deployed into your own environment, with the monitoring and access controls your security team will ask about.

How we work, and what that protects you from

  • Every recommendation carries a number. Build cost, monthly run cost, expected return, payback period. A strategy without those is an opinion, and a board cannot fund an opinion.
  • Your data stays in the EU, and we can prove where. We are an EU company delivering from Romania. Data residency, GDPR posture and a DPA are settled before the work starts, not during your next security review.
  • Model agnostic, by policy. GPT, Claude, Gemini or an open-weight model running inside your own environment. We choose per use case and per data-sensitivity requirement, and we write down why. No vendor relationship decides your architecture.
  • Governance is a deliverable, not a slide. Human-review thresholds, escalation paths, an audit trail, and a written record of why each model was chosen. Enough that you can answer an auditor, an insurer or a customer's procurement team.
  • Nothing we recommend locks you in. You own the code, the prompts, the eval suites and the infrastructure config, it runs in your cloud account, and it is documented for a team that has never met us. Including if that team replaces us.
  • Senior engineers, not a research team. The people advising you build production systems for a living. That is why the estimates hold up, and why we will tell you when something demos far better than it runs.

Our point of view

Most AI strategy work fails at the same point. Not at the analysis — the analysis is usually fine — but at the handover. A document arrives, everyone agrees it is thorough, and nobody has the capacity or the mandate to build any of it. Twelve months later the same board asks the same question.

We think advice you cannot execute is close to worthless, and that a firm which only advises has no incentive to be right. If your estimate is never tested against a real build, being wrong costs you nothing. We publish a cost estimate in week five and then have to live with it in month three, which is a considerably more disciplined way to produce a number.

So we do both, deliberately. The blueprint is a real deliverable you can take elsewhere. But it is written by the people who would build it, which is why it contains integration effort, running costs and failure modes that a pure advisory deck tends to leave out.

Who we are not for: if what you need is a document to satisfy a board — a strategy nobody intends to fund — we are the wrong firm, and a large consultancy will do it better and with a more impressive cover. We are also wrong for you if the AI decision has already been made and you want it validated. We will tell you when a favoured initiative does not pay back, and that is an awkward meeting we are willing to have.

Questions we get asked before we're hired

What does AI consultancy actually cost?

We publish bands, because the alternative wastes everyone's time. A single-function review, the Operational Quick Scan, is typically €4,500–€6,000 over two to three weeks. The full costed strategy, the AI-Ready Ops Blueprint, is typically €9,000–€14,000 over four to six weeks. If you then build, a single workflow taken live as an Automation Pilot typically runs €15,000–€25,000 over six to ten weeks, and a whole department, the Department Automation Sprint, usually sits between €40,000–€65,000 over ten to sixteen weeks. What moves those numbers: how many departments are in scope, how many systems have to be integrated, and the state of your data. More detail in what AI automation costs.

How do we choose between AI consultants?

Three questions separate the field quickly. Ask what they have shipped — not advised on, shipped, with a named client and a system still running. Ask to speak to a client directly, not read a quote in a deck. And ask what went wrong on a recent project and what it cost to fix. A firm that answers the third one specifically has been in production. A firm that gets cagey is telling you something, and it is usually that the delivery happens somewhere they cannot show you. Two more that are worth asking: who exactly will be in the room after the sales call, and will the output include costs or only recommendations.

Are we too small for this?

Probably not. The pattern that makes this work is manual operational load, not headcount — we typically work with companies from around fifty staff upwards, though the real test is whether there is a process consuming meaningful salaried time every week. If you are smaller than that, say so on the call. Sometimes the honest answer is that one integration or an off-the-shelf tool solves it, and we will tell you that instead of scoping an engagement around it.

Our data is a mess. Should we fix that first?

Not before the assessment — the assessment is how you find out how much of it actually matters. In practice you rarely need clean data everywhere. You need it in the two or three places the first initiatives touch, and the blueprint tells you which those are and what fixing them costs. What we will not do is start a build on data we know is unreliable. That is how a pilot demos well and then produces confident nonsense in front of a customer.

Will you build it too, or just advise?

Both, and that is the whole point. A strategy delivered as a PDF and never opened again is worth nothing, and it is the most common outcome in this market. We advise as engineers who will be held to the estimate, then build through AI automation and LLM and agent development if you want us to. There is no obligation. You can take the blueprint to another firm or to your own team, and it is written so that they can price from it.

The board meets in six weeks. Is that realistic?

Yes, that is the window the blueprint is built for. Four to six weeks from kickoff to a costed, ranked plan written to be read by a board rather than by an engineering team. If the meeting is sooner than that, tell us and we will scope a single function instead — two to three weeks, narrower, but a real answer with numbers rather than a holding slide.

Who will we actually be dealing with?

Senior engineers, and the same people throughout. The person who runs your assessment is the person who would build the work afterwards. We have no account manager layer and nothing is handed to a junior team once a contract is signed. If you want to meet them before you commit, ask — we would rather you did. You can also see how we work in full.

What about security, governance and where our data sits?

Your data stays inside the EEA by default. We are an EU company delivering from Romania, a DPA is part of the standard paperwork, and we deploy into your own cloud account rather than ours. Where data cannot leave your environment at all, we run open-weight models inside your own infrastructure instead of calling a hosted API. Governance is a deliverable in its own right: human-review thresholds, escalation paths, model selection criteria and an audit trail, so you can explain any decision to a regulator or an insurer using documents that already exist.

How do we avoid being locked into one AI vendor?

By choosing per use case and writing down why, which is what we do. We are model agnostic and we build so the model behind a workflow can be swapped without rewriting the workflow. You own the code, the prompts, the eval suites and the infrastructure config, and it runs in your account. If a cheaper or better model appears in eighteen months, changing to it should be a configuration decision and a re-run of the eval suite, not a project.

We have already spent money on AI and have nothing to show the board.

This is the most common reason we get called, and the cause is almost always the same. The pilot was picked because it demoed well, not because anyone understood the process behind it, so it worked on clean sample data and fell over on the real thing. Part of the blueprint is auditing what you already have. Occasionally something is worth rescuing and we will say so. More often the honest recommendation is to stop two of the three pilots and fund one properly, which is a defensible thing to take to a board.

Will this be used to cut our team?

That is not what we are engaged to do, and the leadership teams who frame it that way get worse results anyway. The consistent pattern is that the people who run a process become the people who own the automation — they are the only ones who can recognise a wrong output. We involve them from the first week, because a plan built without them is a plan that quietly does not get used. You can see the operations work behind that.

What happens after the blueprint?

Whatever you decide. Some clients build with us, some take it to their own team, and some do nothing for six months and come back. Where we do build, everything ships with evals, guardrails, traces and a cost dashboard, so your team can see it working and see it drift. If what you actually need is senior capacity rather than advice, that is staff augmentation, and if it is purely workflow automation you may want our AI automation agency page instead. ---
