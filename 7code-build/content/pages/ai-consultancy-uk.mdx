## BLOCK: diagnosis

**H2:** You are not short of AI ambition. You are short of a costed answer

Three departments are each running their own pilot. Operations has a tool on a corporate card, finance
is trialling something a vendor demoed well, and someone in marketing has been quietly productive with
a chatbot for months. Nobody has costed any of it, nobody owns it, and none of it is on a risk
register.

Meanwhile the board has asked for an AI strategy by the next meeting. Or worse, money has already gone
out — a pilot, a platform licence, a consultant's deck — and there is nothing to put in front of them
that shows a return.

The problem is not that your team lacks ideas. It is that no one has priced them. AI opportunities all
look equally attractive until someone works out what each costs to build, what it costs to run per
month, what it saves, and what happens when it gets an answer wrong in front of a customer.

That is the work. It takes weeks, not quarters, and it ends in a ranked list with numbers next to it —
including the items we recommend you do not fund.

## BLOCK: pointOfView

**H2:** Our point of view

Most AI strategy work fails at the same point. Not at the analysis — the analysis is usually fine —
but at the handover. A document arrives, everyone agrees it is thorough, and nobody has the capacity
or the mandate to build any of it. Twelve months later the same board asks the same question.

We think advice you cannot execute is close to worthless, and that a firm which only advises has no
incentive to be right. If your estimate is never tested against a real build, being wrong costs you
nothing. We publish a cost estimate in week five and then have to live with it in month three, which
is a considerably more disciplined way to produce a number.

So we do both, deliberately. The blueprint is a real deliverable you can take elsewhere. But it is
written by the people who would build it, which is why it contains integration effort, running costs
and failure modes that a pure advisory deck tends to leave out.

**Who we are not for:** if what you need is a document to satisfy a board — a strategy nobody intends
to fund — we are the wrong firm, and a large consultancy will do it better and with a more impressive
cover. We are also wrong for you if the AI decision has already been made and you want it validated.
We will tell you when a favoured initiative does not pay back, and that is an awkward meeting we are
willing to have.

## BLOCK: faq

**H2:** Questions we get asked before we're hired

**1. What does AI consultancy actually cost?** `objection: price`
We publish bands, because the alternative wastes everyone's time. A single-function review, the
[Operational Quick Scan](/packages/operational-quick-scan), is typically **€4,500–€6,000** over two to
three weeks. The full costed strategy, the [AI-Ready Ops Blueprint](/packages/ai-ready-ops-blueprint),
is typically **€9,000–€14,000** over four to six weeks. If you then build, a single workflow taken live
as an [Automation Pilot](/packages/automation-pilot) typically runs **€15,000–€25,000** over six to ten
weeks, and a whole department, the
[Department Automation Sprint](/packages/department-automation-sprint), usually sits between
**€40,000–€65,000** over ten to sixteen weeks. What moves those numbers: how many departments are in
scope, how many systems have to be integrated, and the state of your data. More detail in
[what AI automation costs](/hub/what-ai-automation-costs-uk).

**2. How do we choose between AI consultants?** `objection: other`
Three questions separate the field quickly. Ask what they have shipped — not advised on, shipped, with
a named client and a system still running. Ask to speak to a client directly, not read a quote in a
deck. And ask what went wrong on a recent project and what it cost to fix. A firm that answers the
third one specifically has been in production. A firm that gets cagey is telling you something, and it
is usually that the delivery happens somewhere they cannot show you. Two more that are worth asking:
who exactly will be in the room after the sales call, and will the output include costs or only
recommendations.

**3. Are we too small for this?** `objection: other`
Probably not. The pattern that makes this work is manual operational load, not headcount — we
typically work with companies from around fifty staff upwards, though the real test is whether there
is a process consuming meaningful salaried time every week. If you are smaller than that, say so on the
call. Sometimes the honest answer is that one integration or an off-the-shelf tool solves it, and we
will tell you that instead of scoping an engagement around it.

**4. Our data is a mess. Should we fix that first?** `objection: other`
Not before the assessment — the assessment is how you find out how much of it actually matters. In
practice you rarely need clean data everywhere. You need it in the two or three places the first
initiatives touch, and the blueprint tells you which those are and what fixing them costs. What we will
not do is start a build on data we know is unreliable. That is how a pilot demos well and then produces
confident nonsense in front of a customer.

**5. Will you build it too, or just advise?** `objection: other`
Both, and that is the whole point. A strategy delivered as a PDF and never opened again is worth
nothing, and it is the most common outcome in this market. We advise as engineers who will be held to
the estimate, then build through [AI automation](/service/ai-automation) and
[LLM and agent development](/service/llm-agent-development) if you want us to. There is no obligation.
You can take the blueprint to another firm or to your own team, and it is written so that they can
price from it.

**6. The board meets in six weeks. Is that realistic?** `objection: timeline`
Yes, that is the window the blueprint is built for. Four to six weeks from kickoff to a costed,
ranked plan written to be read by a board rather than by an engineering team. If the meeting is sooner
than that, tell us and we will scope a single function instead — two to three weeks, narrower, but a
real answer with numbers rather than a holding slide.

**7. Who will we actually be dealing with?** `objection: seniority`
Senior engineers, and the same people throughout. The person who runs your assessment is the person who
would build the work afterwards. We have no account manager layer and nothing is handed to a junior
team once a contract is signed. If you want to meet them before you commit, ask — we would rather you
did. You can also see [how we work](/process) in full.

**8. What about security, governance and where our data sits?** `objection: data-residency`
Your data stays inside the EEA by default. We are an EU company delivering from Romania, a DPA is part
of the standard paperwork, and we deploy into your own cloud account rather than ours. Where data
cannot leave your environment at all, we run open-weight models inside your own infrastructure instead
of calling a hosted API. Governance is a deliverable in its own right: human-review thresholds,
escalation paths, model selection criteria and an audit trail, so you can explain any decision to a
regulator or an insurer using documents that already exist.

**9. How do we avoid being locked into one AI vendor?** `objection: lock-in`
By choosing per use case and writing down why, which is what we do. We are model agnostic and we build
so the model behind a workflow can be swapped without rewriting the workflow. You own the code, the
prompts, the eval suites and the infrastructure config, and it runs in your account. If a cheaper or
better model appears in eighteen months, changing to it should be a configuration decision and a
re-run of the eval suite, not a project.

**10. We have already spent money on AI and have nothing to show the board.** `objection: failed-before`
This is the most common reason we get called, and the cause is almost always the same. The pilot was
picked because it demoed well, not because anyone understood the process behind it, so it worked on
clean sample data and fell over on the real thing. Part of the blueprint is auditing what you already
have. Occasionally something is worth rescuing and we will say so. More often the honest recommendation
is to stop two of the three pilots and fund one properly, which is a defensible thing to take to a
board.

**11. Will this be used to cut our team?** `objection: sideline-our-team`
That is not what we are engaged to do, and the leadership teams who frame it that way get worse results
anyway. The consistent pattern is that the people who run a process become the people who own the
automation — they are the only ones who can recognise a wrong output. We involve them from the first
week, because a plan built without them is a plan that quietly does not get used. You can see the
[operations](/expertise/operations) work behind that.

**12. What happens after the blueprint?** `objection: after-launch`
Whatever you decide. Some clients build with us, some take it to their own team, and some do nothing
for six months and come back. Where we do build, everything ships with evals, guardrails, traces and a
cost dashboard, so your team can see it working and see it drift. If what you actually need is senior
capacity rather than advice, that is [staff augmentation](/staff-augmentation-company-uk), and if it is
purely workflow automation you may want our
[AI automation agency](/ai-automation-agency-uk) page instead.
