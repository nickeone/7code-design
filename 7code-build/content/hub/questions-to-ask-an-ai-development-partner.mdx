## BLOCK: howToUse

**H2:** Why the usual list is useless

Most buying guides tell you to check the portfolio, ask about the process and see whether they
understand your industry. Every agency passes that. The demo will be good, because demos are easy,
and the gap between a demo and a system that survives real users is where projects die.

The questions below are about what happens after the model returns something wrong at 2am on a
Tuesday. A firm that has run AI features in production answers them without preparation. A firm that
has only built prototypes answers them with philosophy.

Use them in a technical call, not a sales call. You are not testing whether they can say the words.
You are testing for specifics: a number, a tool, a story about the time it went wrong.

One fair warning. No supplier passes every question perfectly, and a firm that claims to should worry
you more than one admitting a gap. You want a pattern of concrete answers and honesty about the
edges. Our [AI development company](/ai-development-company-uk) page sets out how we scope and
deliver, if you want the shape of a full engagement first.

## BLOCK: questions

**H2:** The fifteen questions

### 1. Which engineer will write this code, and can I meet them this week?

**Why it matters.** The single most common failure in agency work is the bait and switch: senior
people sell it, juniors build it. This question surfaces that in one move, because a firm that
intends to hand your project to whoever is free cannot produce a name.

**A good answer sounds like:** a name, a short summary of what that person last shipped, and an offer
to put them on a call in the next few days. Better still, they were already on your first call.

**A bad answer sounds like:** "we allocate from our talent pool based on availability at kickoff", or
a promise that you will meet the team after signature.

### 2. What does your eval suite look like for a feature like mine?

**Why it matters.** Evals are the automated tests of AI engineering. Without them, nobody can tell
whether a change improved the system or quietly broke it. A firm that does not run evals is shipping
on vibes and will be unable to tell you why quality dropped.

**A good answer sounds like:** they describe a labelled dataset built from your real data, a scoring
method per task type, a threshold that blocks a release, and how the set grows when a bug is found in
production. They will tell you how many examples they typically start with and why.

**A bad answer sounds like:** "we test it thoroughly", "our prompts are well engineered", or eval
described as something they do at the end before handover.

### 3. How do you measure retrieval quality, separately from answer quality?

**Why it matters.** In a retrieval system, most wrong answers are retrieval failures wearing a
generation costume. If you only measure the final answer, you cannot tell whether to fix the index,
the chunking, the query rewriting or the prompt. Teams that conflate the two debug by guessing.

**A good answer sounds like:** they measure whether the right document made it into context at all,
before judging the answer. They will name the metrics they use, explain how they built the set of
question-to-correct-document pairs, and describe what they changed when recall was poor. Anyone doing
serious [RAG development](/rag-development-company) has these numbers.

**A bad answer sounds like:** "we use a vector database", as if the choice of database were the
answer. Naming a tool is not naming a measurement.

### 4. What is your guardrail strategy, and what specifically does it block?

**Why it matters.** Guardrails are where legal and brand risk live. Vague ones are worse than none,
because they create the impression of safety without a specification anyone can check.

**A good answer sounds like:** a layered description. Input validation, output schema enforcement,
refusal handling for out-of-scope questions, a check on anything leaving the system, and a defined
behaviour when a guardrail fires — retry, escalate, or fail closed. They should also say what they
deliberately do not block, and why over-blocking has a cost.

**A bad answer sounds like:** "the model provider handles safety". That covers their risk, not yours.

### 5. Who owns the prompts, and where do they live?

**Why it matters.** Prompts are product logic. If they live in a proprietary platform you cannot
export, you have bought a dependency, not a system. If they live inside code with no versioning, you
cannot roll one back or run an A/B comparison.

**A good answer sounds like:** you own them, they are versioned in your repository, they are
reviewable in a pull request, and changing one triggers the eval suite. Prompt changes are treated as
code changes because they are.

**A bad answer sounds like:** prompts held in the agency's tooling, or "we keep them internally
because they are our secret sauce". Your business logic is not their intellectual property.

### 6. How do you attribute token cost per feature?

**Why it matters.** An unattributed AI bill is a bill you cannot manage. When spend triples you need
to know which feature did it, and whether it came from more usage or a worse prompt. Without
attribution, your only control is turning things off.

**A good answer sounds like:** every call tagged by feature, tenant and environment, cost visible per
run in a dashboard, and an alert when spend per unit of work moves. They can quote the cost of a
single transaction, not just the monthly total.

**A bad answer sounds like:** "we monitor the provider console". That is one aggregate number and it
tells you nothing about which part of your product is expensive.

### 7. What happens when the model we build on is deprecated?

**Why it matters.** Providers retire models on their own schedule. If your system is welded to one
model's exact behaviour, that announcement is an emergency. This question tests whether the
architecture anticipates something that is certain to happen.

**A good answer sounds like:** an abstraction layer over the provider, evals that can be re-run
against a candidate replacement to quantify the difference, and a migration process they have
actually performed. They should be candid that migration is real work, not free.

**A bad answer sounds like:** "we always use the latest model", or a claim that swapping models is
trivial. Anyone who has done it knows prompts do not transfer cleanly.

### 8. What is your rollback plan when a change makes output worse?

**Why it matters.** Quality regressions in AI systems are often silent. Nothing throws an error; the
answers just get slightly worse. You need to know how they detect that and how fast they can undo it.

**A good answer sounds like:** prompts, model version and configuration are all versioned together
and deployable as a unit, releases go out behind a flag or to a subset of traffic first, and there is
a documented path back that has been rehearsed. They should quote a time: minutes, not days.

**A bad answer sounds like:** rollback described only for application code, with prompts and model
configuration edited live in a console.

### 9. How do you handle data residency, and what exactly leaves our environment?

**Why it matters.** Most teams cannot answer precisely which data reaches which third party. That is
a problem in any regulated context, and an awkward one in a supplier due-diligence questionnaire.

**A good answer sounds like:** a specific data-flow answer. What is sent to the model provider, in
what form, whether it is redacted first, which region processes it, what is retained and for how
long, and what the alternative is when data cannot leave your environment at all. They should raise
the option of running an open-weight model in your own infrastructure without being prompted.

**A bad answer sounds like:** "it is all encrypted and GDPR compliant". Encryption in transit is not
a residency answer, and compliance is not a description of where data goes.

### 10. What will you refuse to build with an LLM?

**Why it matters.** A firm that will put a model into anything is optimising for the invoice. Serious
engineers have a list of things that should be plain deterministic code, and they will argue for it
against their own commercial interest.

**A good answer sounds like:** arithmetic, anything requiring exact reproducibility, decisions with a
hard legal or financial consequence and no human review, and any rule that can be written as a rule.
They will suggest hybrid designs where the model handles the fuzzy part and code handles the rest.

**A bad answer sounds like:** enthusiasm without limits. If everything is an agent, nothing has been
thought about.

### 11. How do you write acceptance criteria for a non-deterministic system?

**Why it matters.** Traditional acceptance testing assumes the same input gives the same output.
Models do not work that way. If nobody has solved this contractually, you will argue about whether
the thing works at the exact moment you are due to pay for it.

**A good answer sounds like:** acceptance defined statistically against a labelled set that you have
seen and agreed. A stated pass rate per task category, an agreed behaviour for the failing tail, and
a named person on your side who signs off the test set before the build starts.

**A bad answer sounds like:** "you will know it when you see it", or a demo used as acceptance.

### 12. What does your production observability look like?

**Why it matters.** When a user says the assistant gave a bad answer last Thursday, someone has to
reconstruct exactly what happened. Without traces, that investigation is impossible and every bug
report becomes an argument.

**A good answer sounds like:** every run is traced end to end — inputs, retrieved context, tool calls,
model output, latency, cost — with the trace linkable from a support ticket. They will tell you the
retention period and how personal data is masked in the traces.

**A bad answer sounds like:** application logs only, or a dashboard that shows request counts and
error rates but not what the model actually saw.

### 13. Tell me about a project that went badly, and what you changed afterwards.

**Why it matters.** This is the fairness question, and it is the most revealing one on the list.
Everybody has a project that went wrong. A firm that cannot describe one is either very new or not
being straight with you. You are listening for ownership rather than blame, and for a specific change
to how they work.

**A good answer sounds like:** a real situation, a clear description of what they misjudged, what it
cost, and the concrete practice they adopted afterwards. Good firms are comfortable here. It tends to
be the moment the conversation becomes honest.

**A bad answer sounds like:** a story where the client was the problem, or a non-answer dressed as
discretion. Confidentiality prevents naming a client. It does not prevent describing a mistake.

### 14. Who owns the IP, and at what moment does it transfer?

**Why it matters.** "You own everything" is easy to say and often untrue in the contract. The details
matter: their internal frameworks, third-party components, the model weights if anything was
fine-tuned, and the timing of transfer.

**A good answer sounds like:** a clear statement of what transfers and when, usually on full payment,
with any retained internal tooling named and licensed to you. They will point to the clause rather
than paraphrase it. Anything built on their own scaffolding is called out explicitly.

**A bad answer sounds like:** a verbal assurance not reflected in the draft agreement, or silence
about pre-existing components embedded in your system.

### 15. What does handover look like if we take this in-house in six months?

**Why it matters.** The best test of whether a supplier is building for you or for their retainer.
Ask it early, while it is hypothetical and nobody is defensive.

**A good answer sounds like:** an actual list. Repository access, infrastructure as code in your
cloud account, runbooks, the eval suite and its dataset, prompt history, an architecture decision
record, and a paid handover period with your engineers shadowing theirs. They will treat it as a
normal outcome rather than a threat.

**A bad answer sounds like:** hesitation, or a handover fee that appears for the first time in this
conversation. If a supplier cannot describe how you leave, you should assume leaving is difficult.

## BLOCK: fairness

**H2:** Being fair to the supplier

A checklist can be used badly. Three notes so this one is not.

**Do not expect fifteen perfect answers.** A small firm may not run per-feature cost attribution on
every project. What matters is whether the gaps are acknowledged and what they would do instead.

**Weight the questions to your risk.** For an internal tool used by twelve people, guardrails and
rollback matter less than speed. Where output touches customers or regulated decisions, questions
four, nine and eleven are the whole conversation.

**Give them the context to answer well.** Several of these have no good answer in the abstract.
Retrieval quality depends on your corpus; cost attribution depends on your architecture. Send the
problem in advance. A considered answer teaches you more than an ambush.

## BLOCK: scorecard

**H2:** How to score the conversation

Three signals separate the firms worth shortlisting, whatever the individual answers were.

**Specificity.** Good answers contain nouns: a tool, a metric, a threshold, a number. Weak answers
contain adjectives. Count them.

**Volunteered limits.** A supplier who tells you what they would not build, or where their approach
gets expensive, is describing real experience. Nobody who has shipped this work thinks it is easy.

**Consistency between the call and the contract.** Everything said about IP, handover, data residency
and ownership should appear in the draft agreement without you asking. If it does not, the call was
marketing.

## BLOCK: faq

**H2:** Questions we get asked before we're hired

**1. How many suppliers should I run this with?** `objection: other`
Three is usually enough, and more than four wastes everyone's time including yours. Run the same
questions with each, in the same order, and write the answers down during the call. The differences
are obvious on paper and much less obvious from memory a week later.

**2. Can I ask these if I am not technical?** `objection: seniority`
Yes, and you should. You do not need to evaluate the technical content to evaluate the answer. Note
whether they gave you something specific, whether they explained it in language you followed, and
whether they were comfortable being pinned down. Bring a technical colleague or advisor for the
follow-up if you have one.

**3. What if a supplier refuses to let me meet the engineer?** `objection: seniority`
Treat it as an answer. There are legitimate scheduling reasons for a delay of a few days, but not for
a refusal. The person who will build the system should be reachable before you sign, and at 7code
they are usually already on the first call.

**4. Are these questions unfair to smaller firms?** `objection: other`
No, and small firms often do better on them. A two-person team can name the engineer immediately and
usually knows their own cost per transaction. Where small suppliers struggle is continuity, so add a
question about what happens if their lead engineer is unavailable for a month.

**5. Should I send the list in advance?** `objection: other`
Send it. You are hiring for engineering judgement, not improvisation. A prepared answer with real
artefacts behind it beats a fluent one invented on the call.

**6. What if their answers are good but the price is high?** `objection: price`
Compare what is included rather than the headline. Evals, guardrails, traces and cost dashboards are
either in the quote or they are work your team does later. A cheaper quote that omits them is a
deferred invoice, not a saving.

**7. How do I check what they tell me?** `objection: other`
Ask for a reference from a client whose system is still in production a year later, and put the
rollback and handover questions to them. Review platforms help with the relationship questions.
Neither substitutes for a technical conversation with the engineer.

**8. Do you mind being asked these?** `objection: other`
Not at all. We wrote them. Buyers who ask hard questions early make good clients later, because
expectations are set before there is a contract to protect.
