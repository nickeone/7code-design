## BLOCK: adequacy

**H2:** The UK–EU position, in practical terms

Since Brexit the UK and the EU run two parallel data protection regimes that started from the same
text. The practical question is narrow: what has to be in place before personal data moves from your
systems to a supplier inside the European Economic Area?

For most of the period since Brexit that transfer has been treated as unrestricted in both
directions, because each side recognised the other as offering an adequate level of protection.
Adequacy decisions are time-limited, subject to review, and can be amended or withdrawn. Buyers
routinely treat adequacy as a permanent fact rather than a status with an expiry date.

So the answer is conditional. **If adequacy is in force when you transfer**, no additional mechanism
is needed and the work is about the contract and the security posture. **If it is not**, you fall
back to a mechanism such as standard contractual clauses plus a documented transfer risk assessment.
Have your counsel confirm the current status before relying on either path.

Two consequences either way. Sensible suppliers sign the contractual clauses regardless, so a change
in status does not stop the project. And adequacy settles only the legality of the transfer, not
whether the arrangement is well run.

This is one of the quieter arguments for [nearshore software development](/nearshore-software-development-uk)
inside the EEA. The transfer conversation is shorter, and the supplier sits inside a regime your
counsel already understands.

## BLOCK: dpa

**H2:** What a data processing agreement actually has to do

If a supplier handles personal data on your behalf, you are the controller and they are the
processor. That relationship is documented in a data processing agreement, usually a schedule to the
main contract. Most suppliers have one. Fewer have one that describes what they actually do.

A DPA doing its job covers the following in specifics rather than boilerplate:

- **Subject matter, duration, nature and purpose.** What data, whose, for how long, to do what.
- **Categories of data subject and personal data**, noting explicitly whether special-category data is
  involved, because that changes the risk profile and often the architecture.
- **Instructions.** The processor acts only on your documented instructions. This clause is what
  stops a supplier using your data for something you did not ask for.
- **Confidentiality of personnel**, covering everyone with access.
- **Security measures.** The part most often left vague. Access control, encryption, environment
  separation, logging and retention, as actually implemented.
- **Sub-processors** — who else touches the data, and how you are told before a new one is added.
- **Assistance obligations** — data subject requests, breach notification within an agreed window,
  support for impact assessments.
- **Deletion or return at the end**, including copies in backups and development environments.
- **Audit and information rights**, and the practical form they take.

A useful test: read the security section and ask whether an engineer could implement your system from
it. If it could describe any supplier in any industry, it is decoration.

## BLOCK: subProcessors

**H2:** Sub-processors, and why the model provider is one

This is where AI projects differ from ordinary software projects, and where older DPA templates fall
over.

When your supplier builds a feature that sends text to a hosted model API, that provider is
processing your data. In practical terms it is a sub-processor of your processor, and it belongs on
the sub-processor list alongside the cloud host, the error tracker and the logging platform.

**The list must be current and specific.** Naming the provider is not enough. Which service, which
region, under which commercial terms? Consumer and business tiers of the same product frequently
differ on retention and training.

**Training and retention have to be pinned down.** Business and enterprise tiers of the major
providers generally do not train on customer content by default, and offer configurable retention.
Defaults differ and change with contract terms, so confirm this against the agreement your supplier
actually holds, not a marketing page.

**Logging is the quiet leak.** Prompts and completions often land in an observability tool, an error
tracker or a support ticket. That is a second copy, in a second system, frequently in another region
with longer retention than the model provider itself. Trace masking should be a stated requirement.

Ask for the data-flow diagram of one AI feature, showing every third party a payload reaches. If it
cannot be produced in a day, nobody has done the exercise.

## BLOCK: residencyVsProcessing

**H2:** Data residency is not the same as data processing

The two terms get used interchangeably in sales conversations. They mean different things.

**Residency** is where data sits at rest: a database in an EU region, backups in an EU region. Easy
to configure, easy to evidence.

**Processing** is everywhere data is handled in flight, including by people. The region inference
runs in, the region logs land in, the laptop of the engineer debugging production, and any support
tooling in between.

A supplier can honestly say your data is stored in the EU while inference happens elsewhere. Both
statements can be true at once. Ask about each separately, in writing.

Two related distinctions matter. **Personal data is not the same as your data** — commercially
sensitive information may carry none, and it is your contract rather than data protection law that
protects it. And **production is not the same as development**: many leaks are simply a copy of a
production database sitting in a test environment so someone could reproduce a bug. How a supplier
answers on test data tells you more than the residency section of their DPA.

## BLOCK: hostedVsOpenWeight

**H2:** Hosted model APIs versus open-weight models in your own environment

Every AI project makes this choice, deliberately or by default. The trade is capability against
control.

**Hosted APIs** give you the strongest models, no infrastructure to run and fast iteration. Most
offer regional processing and enterprise terms covering retention and training. For most business
workloads this is the right answer, and the compliance work is contractual: confirm the region, the
retention and the training position, then record it in the sub-processor list.

**Open-weight models in your own environment** mean data never leaves infrastructure you control. No
third-party sub-processor, no cross-border question at inference, full control over logging. The
costs are real: you run the hardware or managed instances, and on the hardest reasoning tasks the
quality gap can matter.

In practice the answer is usually a hybrid. Classification, extraction, routing and summarisation
over sensitive documents run well on a smaller open-weight model in your own environment. The
genuinely difficult tasks go to a hosted API, with sensitive fields stripped first.

Three engineering points decide whether that works. Redaction happens before the call, because
removing an identifier from a stored response does not unsend it. A provider abstraction layer is
worth building early, so a change in the compliance answer does not mean rewriting the feature. And
evals make the choice measurable: one labelled test set against both options compares accuracy
against control with numbers rather than opinions.

We are model agnostic for this reason. The model is chosen per use case and per data-sensitivity
requirement, not by a vendor relationship.

## BLOCK: ipOwnership

**H2:** IP ownership when your supplier is an EU company

A common worry: if the work is done in Romania, does the client really own it?

The mechanics are less exotic than the anxiety suggests. Ownership of what is created for you is
determined by the contract and by the law it says governs it. Jurisdictions differ on who owns work
made by an employee or contractor absent an agreement. That is why the agreement exists.

Six things to check in a supplier's paperwork, and to have counsel confirm.

**Does assignment cover everything, present and future?** Code, designs, documentation, prompts, eval
datasets, infrastructure definitions. Prompts and eval sets are missing from older templates and are
now core product assets.

**When does transfer happen?** Usually on full payment, which is normal. What matters is that the
trigger is unambiguous and you know what you hold before it fires.

**Does the supplier's team assign to the supplier?** The chain matters. Employment contracts should
assign work product upward, so the supplier has something to pass to you. If any part of the team is
subcontracted, the same must hold one level down.

**What is retained, and under what licence?** Every experienced firm reuses internal scaffolding, and
that makes your project cheaper. Discovering after handover that a component you depend on is not
yours does not. Retained items should be named and licensed to you perpetually and irrevocably.

**Open-source components.** Ask for a dependency inventory with licences. Awkward to reconstruct
later.

**Governing law and forum.** Often English law for a UK client, sometimes the supplier's. Both work.
It should be a conscious choice rather than whatever the template said.

## BLOCK: ndas

**H2:** NDAs, and what they do not cover

Sign one before the first detailed technical conversation. Any serious supplier will.

**Mutual beats one-way.** You will see their methods and internal tooling as well as the reverse, and
a mutual agreement is quicker to negotiate because nobody is arguing about asymmetry.

**An NDA is not a DPA and not an IP assignment.** It governs confidentiality between two businesses.
It says nothing about who owns the code, and it does not make a transfer of personal data lawful. It
is the first of three documents, not a substitute for the other two.

## BLOCK: whatToAsk

**H2:** What to actually ask a supplier

Ten questions. Written answers, before contract, not reassurance on a call.

1. Where is personal data stored at rest, and in which region is it processed in flight?
2. The full sub-processor list for my engagement, including the model provider's specific service and
   region.
3. Under which commercial terms is your model provider account held, and what are the retention and
   training positions under them?
4. A data-flow diagram for one AI feature, marking every third party a payload reaches.
5. What appears in traces and logs, how is it masked, where does it live, and for how long?
6. Do your engineers work with production data? Under what controls, or how is test data generated?
7. Which transfer mechanism does the contract rely on, and what happens if adequacy changes mid-engagement?
8. The IP clause, the transfer trigger, and the list of retained internal components.
9. What is your breach notification window to us, and who makes the call?
10. How is data deleted at the end, including backups and development environments, and how is that
    evidenced?

Quick, specific answers mean the supplier has done this before. A certification badge and a paragraph
about taking security seriously means keep asking.

We settle all ten during scoping rather than mid-build. The sequence is on [how we work](/process),
and where it fits a broader engagement on our [AI consultancy](/ai-consultancy-uk) page.

## BLOCK: faq

**H2:** Questions we get asked before we're hired

**1. Is it still straightforward for a UK company to use an EU supplier after Brexit?** `objection: data-residency`
In day-to-day practice, yes, and it is common. The position depends on the adequacy status in force
at the time, which is reviewed periodically and can change. Sensible suppliers keep standard
contractual clauses in the pack as a fallback. Have your counsel confirm the current position rather
than relying on an article.

**2. Where does our data sit if we work with you?** `objection: data-residency`
Inside the EEA by default. We are a Romanian company delivering from Cluj-Napoca, and we deploy into
your own cloud account in an EU region unless you say otherwise. Where data cannot leave your
environment at all, we run open-weight models inside your infrastructure.

**3. Do you sign our DPA or do we sign yours?** `objection: other`
Either. Most clients prefer their own, which is sensible when they are the controller. We review it,
flag anything that does not match what we actually do, and sign.

**4. Does the model provider train on our data?** `objection: data-residency`
Under business and enterprise terms the major providers generally do not train on customer content by
default, and offer configurable retention. Terms differ and change, so we confirm it in writing
against the agreement in place for your project and record it in the sub-processor list.

**5. Who owns the code and when?** `objection: ip-ownership`
You do, transferring on full payment. That covers code, designs, documentation, prompts, eval
datasets and infrastructure definitions. Anything built on our internal scaffolding is named
explicitly and licensed to you for use in the delivered system.

**6. Can you complete our security questionnaire and sign an NDA first?** `objection: other`
Yes to both, and we prefer a mutual NDA. Send the questionnaire before the technical call. Anything
we cannot answer we mark as such rather than guess.

**7. What happens to our data when the engagement ends?** `objection: after-launch`
Access is revoked and working copies are deleted on the schedule agreed in the DPA, including
development environments and backups. Because we deploy into your cloud account, production data was
in your control throughout.

**8. Should we run a data protection impact assessment?** `objection: other`
That is a question for your own adviser. Where one is needed we supply the technical detail: data
flows, sub-processors, retention, security measures. We do not decide whether it is required.

**9. Does any of this slow a project down?** `objection: timeline`
Only if it starts late. Handled during scoping it costs no calendar time. Handled mid-build, a
security review can hold a project for weeks.
