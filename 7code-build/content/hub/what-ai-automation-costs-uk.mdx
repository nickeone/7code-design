**H2:** The short answer, before the caveats

Most buyers get told "it depends" and a discovery call. Here is the actual shape of the market as we
quote it. These are the bands we work in, in euros, for UK and EU clients.

| What you are buying | Duration | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Operational Quick Scan** — map the processes, rank them, cost them | 2–3 weeks | **€4,500 – €6,000** |
| **AI-Ready Ops Blueprint** — target architecture, data readiness, sequencing | 4–6 weeks | **€9,000 – €14,000** |
| **Automation Pilot** — one workflow automated end to end, in production | 6–10 weeks | **€15,000 – €25,000** |
| **Department Automation Sprint** — a whole function digitised | 10–16 weeks | **€40,000 – €65,000** |
| **Production MVP** — a new AI product, not an internal automation | 3–5 months | **€40,000 – €90,000** |
| **Dedicated senior engineer** — embedded in your team | monthly | **€6,400 – €8,000** |
| **Dedicated architect** | monthly | **€9,600 – €12,800** |
| **Dedicated pod** — architect plus two senior engineers | monthly | **€22,000 – €29,000** |

Two caveats, and they matter more than the table.

First, these are build costs. They do not include what the automation costs to run each month, which
is a separate and much smaller number. We cover that further down.

Second, the range inside each band is not padding. A €15,000 pilot and a €25,000 pilot are different
projects. The next section is about what decides which one you have.

If you want the wider picture of how this work gets scoped and delivered, start with our

## BLOCK: costDrivers

**H2:** What actually moves the number

Six things decide where you land in a band. In our experience they account for nearly all the
variance between two projects that sound identical in a first call.

**1. How many systems the workflow touches.** One system with a documented API is straightforward.
Four systems, one of which is a 2011 ERP with a CSV export and no test environment, is a different
project. Integration work is the single largest cost driver in [AI automation](/service/ai-automation),
and it is almost always underestimated because it is invisible in the process diagram.

**2. Whether the process is actually defined.** Many processes exist as habits, not rules. Ask five
people how an invoice exception is handled and you get five answers. Automating that means deciding
the rule first, which is a business decision, not an engineering one. It takes meeting time, and
meeting time with the right people is the scarcest thing in these projects.

**3. The cost of being wrong.** An automation that drafts an internal summary needs light checking.
One that issues a credit note, files a regulatory return, or emails a customer needs review steps,
audit trails, and a tested rollback. The accuracy bar you set drives the amount of engineering
around the model, and that scaffolding usually costs more than the model call itself.

**4. Data quality and access.** Clean, structured, accessible data makes a project cheap. Scanned
PDFs, inconsistent reference codes, duplicated customer records and a data owner on annual leave make
it expensive. This is worth diagnosing before you commit to a build, not during one.

**5. How many exceptions you want handled.** Automating the eighty per cent of cases that follow the
rule is fast. The remaining twenty per cent is where most of the budget goes. Often the right answer
is to route exceptions to a person and stop there, but that has to be an explicit decision.

**6. Whether it has to be real time.** A nightly batch job is simpler, cheaper and easier to fix than
a live event-driven pipeline. Ask honestly whether anyone would notice a four-hour delay. Frequently
nobody would, and the answer saves a meaningful share of the build.

None of these are AI-specific. That is the point. The model is rarely the hard part, and
[system integrations](/service/system-integrations) usually are.

## BLOCK: workedExample

**H2:** One workflow, costed line by line

Here is a realistic shape for a mid-range [Automation Pilot](/packages/automation-pilot). The
workflow: inbound supplier invoices arrive by email, someone opens each one, keys the values into a
finance system, checks them against a purchase order, and escalates mismatches.

The split below is illustrative of how we build a quote. The total is the published band, and the
proportions shift by project.

| Line item | What it covers | Share of the build |
|---|---|---|
| Process definition and rules | Sitting with the finance team, writing down what actually happens, agreeing the exception rules | ~10% |
| Data and document handling | Ingesting the email, extracting fields from mixed-quality PDFs, normalising supplier references | ~20% |
| Integration | Reading purchase orders and writing entries into the finance system, including a test environment that may not exist yet | ~25% |
| Decision logic and model work | Prompting, structured output, confidence scoring, choosing what the model decides and what plain code decides | ~15% |
| Evals and test data | A labelled set of real invoices, an automated scoring run, a threshold below which the release does not ship | ~10% |
| Human-in-the-loop interface | The review queue where a person confirms or corrects a low-confidence extraction | ~10% |
| Observability, guardrails, rollback | Traces, a cost dashboard, alerting on drift, and the switch that puts the process back to manual | ~10% |

Total: **€15,000 – €25,000**, six to ten weeks, one workflow live in production.

Two things are worth noticing. The model work is roughly one-seventh of the project. And the last
three lines, the ones that make it survivable, are about a third. A quote that omits them is not
cheaper. It has moved the cost to the month after go-live, when your team is the one absorbing it.

## BLOCK: overruns

**H2:** What makes these projects go over

Overruns in this work are boringly predictable. We have seen the same five causes repeatedly, and
four of them are avoidable before a contract is signed.

**Access arrives late.** The build is quoted assuming credentials, a sandbox and sample data in week
one. They arrive in week four because a security review was never scheduled. Nothing was
mis-estimated; the calendar moved. Book the access conversation before kickoff.

**The process changes underneath the build.** Operations restructures the approval chain in week
five. That is a legitimate business decision, and it invalidates work already done. Freeze the
process for the pilot, or accept a change order.

**Edge cases surface as requirements.** "It also needs to handle credit notes" is a new project, not
a detail. Write the exclusion list into the scope explicitly, and review it in week two rather than
week eight.

**Nobody owns the outcome on your side.** A project with no single named owner drifts into
consensus-seeking. Decisions that take an hour with an owner take three weeks in a committee. This is
the most expensive failure mode we see, and it has nothing to do with technology.

**The accuracy bar moves once people see the output.** A team asks for eighty per cent, sees the
remaining twenty, and wants ninety-five. That is a reasonable reaction and a genuine change of scope.
Agree the bar against a labelled test set at the start, not against a demo.

## BLOCK: howToReduce

**H2:** How to spend less without getting less

Seven levers, roughly in order of how much they save.

**Scan before you build.** The cheapest project is the one you correctly decided not to do. Two to
three weeks of process mapping regularly removes a six-figure build from the plan, or replaces it
with a much smaller one.

**Automate one workflow, not a department.** A single pilot proves the pattern, exposes the data
problems and gives your team something to react to. Buying the department sprint first means making
every scoping decision before you have learned anything.

**Use code where code works.** A lot of what gets sold as AI automation is a rules engine with better
marketing. If a decision can be expressed as a rule, write the rule. It is cheaper to build, cheaper
to run, and it does not need an eval suite. Reserve the model for genuinely fuzzy judgement.

**Pick a process with one system of record.** Cost tracks integration count almost linearly. A
workflow living inside one platform can be a third of the price of the same logic spanning four.

**Accept batch over real time.** Ask what breaks if this runs every hour instead of every second.
Usually nothing does, and the architecture gets simpler.

**Let a person handle the tail.** Routing the hardest five per cent to a human is not a failure. It
is frequently the highest-return design decision in the whole project.

**Own the infrastructure.** Running the automation in your own cloud account, with your own model
provider contract, means no per-seat platform fee on top and no renegotiation when volume grows. It
also means you can change supplier without changing systems.

## BLOCK: runningCosts

**H2:** What it costs to run, once it is live

Build cost is the number everyone asks about. Running cost is the number that surprises people, in
both directions. It has three parts.

**Model API spend.** Charged per token by whichever provider you use. Published prices change often
and have fallen repeatedly, so we will not quote a figure that will be wrong within months. Estimate
it directly: average tokens in and out per run, multiplied by your provider's current published rate,
multiplied by expected monthly volume. Do this during the pilot on real traffic. For most internal
back-office workflows it lands far below the cost of the hours it replaces.

**Infrastructure.** Compute, storage, queues, logging. Ordinary cloud cost in your own account,
usually modest for a single workflow. Running an open-weight model on your own hardware moves cost
from per-token to per-hour, worth modelling if volume is high or data cannot leave your environment.

**Maintenance.** APIs change, document formats change, and model behaviour changes when a provider
ships a new version. Budget engineering time, either as a retainer or in-house. An eval suite makes
maintenance cheap, because you learn something drifted from a failing test rather than a complaint.

We ship a cost dashboard with every automation for this reason. You should see spend per workflow,
per run, without asking an engineer.

## BLOCK: faq

**H2:** Questions we get asked before we're hired

**1. What is the smallest useful project?** `objection: price`
A process scan, **typically €4,500–€6,000** over two to three weeks. Below that you are buying an
opinion, not an assessment. If you already know exactly which workflow to automate and have clean
system access, the [Automation Pilot](/packages/automation-pilot) at **€15,000–€25,000** is the
smallest thing that reaches production.

**2. Why quote in euros when we are a UK company?** `objection: other`
We are an EU company and we contract in euros. Clients budget in sterling and pay in euros, or we
agree a fixed sterling equivalent at signature so the number does not move mid-project. Either works,
and neither changes the scope.

**3. Is a fixed price or time and materials better?** `objection: price`
For a scoped pilot, fixed. You know the number, we carry the estimation risk, and the scope is small
enough to define properly. For ongoing product work, a monthly team is honest and a fixed price is
theatre. A [dedicated senior engineer](/ai-automation-agency-uk) runs **€6,400–€8,000** monthly and a
pod of an architect plus two senior engineers **€22,000–€29,000**.

**4. How does this compare to hiring someone?** `objection: price`
An automation pilot is a one-off cost that keeps working. A hire is a recurring cost with recruitment
time attached, and one person rarely covers engineering, integration and operations. That said, if
you need continuous automation work for years, an internal team is the right answer and we will say
so.

**5. What is the ongoing cost after the build?** `objection: after-launch`
Model spend, infrastructure and maintenance. Model spend is metered per token by your provider and is
usually the smallest of the three for internal workflows. Infrastructure runs in your own cloud
account. Maintenance is either a retainer with us or time from your own engineers. We ship a cost
dashboard so you can see the running number rather than estimate it.

**6. Do you charge for the scoping call?** `objection: price`
No. The first call is free and produces a shortlist of processes worth looking at. The paid work
starts at the scan, where we go into your systems and produce something written that you own.

**7. What if the scan says do not build anything?** `objection: other`
Then it says that, and it is a good outcome. It has happened. The alternative is discovering the same
thing four months into a build. An agency paid only to build is structurally unlikely to give you
that answer, which is exactly why the scan is a separate, small, paid engagement.

**8. Can we start bigger than a pilot?** `objection: timeline`
Yes, and some clients do. The [Department Automation Sprint](/packages/department-automation-sprint)
covers a whole function over ten to sixteen weeks, **typically €40,000–€65,000**. We only recommend
starting there when a previous pilot has already proved the data and the integrations. Otherwise you
are making every scoping decision blind.

**9. Do you need a long-term contract?** `objection: lock-in`
No. Each engagement stands alone. You own the code, the prompts, the eval suites and the
infrastructure config, and it runs in your cloud account. Some clients keep us on for the next
workflow; plenty take it in house after the first pilot, and that is a fine outcome.

**10. What is not included in these numbers?** `objection: price`
Third-party software licences, your own model provider spend, and your team's time. We are explicit
about all three in a quote. We also exclude anything on the written exclusion list, which we agree in
week two rather than discovering in week eight. You can see the full sequence in
[how we work](/process).
